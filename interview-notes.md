# Clarivi: interview notes

*My product story, kept up to date as I build. Last updated 8 October 2026, during Phase 6 (hardening, before testers join).*

## Who it's for and why

I train regularly and I wear my Apple Watch every night, so I have years of heart rate, heart rate variability and sleep data. The watch is very good at recording numbers and very bad at telling me what they mean for today. I'd wake up to a heart rate variability number and have no idea whether it was bad for me or just a normal wobble.

So I built Clarivi for people like me: Apple Watch users who train, wear the watch overnight, and want to understand their numbers rather than read isolated daily values. Each morning it gives a plain-words answer: what last night's readings mean against your own normal, why today's status was set, and one action for today. The one-line pitch is "Apple Watch records the numbers; this tool explains them."

Version 1 is deliberately small: me plus three testers, for a four-week test.

## How I scoped it

I started by writing down the three things the MVP had to prove, because anything that didn't serve one of them was a candidate for cutting:

- **Feasibility:** can data get from the watch to the tool every morning without anyone doing anything?
- **Signal:** does the status flag the days that were really off, and stay quiet on normal days?
- **Value:** does the nudge actually change someone's decision some of the time?

I set the success targets before building anything, and froze them so I couldn't move the goalposts once results came in. For example: a successful morning sync on at least 26 of 28 days, and on my own backfilled year, at least 2 in 3 objectively disrupted days (travel, illness, very short workouts) should have been called "ease off" or "rest". With only four people, I'm honest that this shows direction, not proof, so I paired the numbers with an end-of-test conversation with each tester.

The hardest scoping call was cutting the causal layer (guided self-experiments). It was the part I found most interesting, but it needs far more history than a four-week test gives. I kept its data structures in the design so it can be built later without rework, and I'll present it as method rather than as a live result.

## Key decisions and tradeoffs

- **An installable web app, not a native iPhone app.** A standalone app would have meant the App Store route and Apple's paid developer membership, which I ruled out in my plan. A web app added to the Home Screen avoids both, and since iOS 16.4 it can receive push notifications. The tradeoff is iOS quirks: notifications only work once the app is on the Home Screen, and the Home Screen app keeps its sign-in separate from Safari. I designed onboarding around that.
- **Accepting the cost of that choice when it bit.** I also wanted my sleep data to sync to Clarivi automatically, the moment the Health app had last night's sleep. Once I was using it every morning, needing to unplug my phone and then open an app felt clunky. But only a native iPhone app can react to new Health data, and the iPhone locks Health data while the phone is locked, so a Shortcut has to run while I'm using the phone. Rather than reopen the web-app decision days before the test, I kept it and took the friction out where I could: a Sync now button on the morning card, shown while last night isn't in yet, that runs the sync in one tap. I chose that over widening the automation to more apps.
- **An iPhone Shortcut to move the data, not a HealthKit app or manual exports.** Nothing to install, and it runs automatically each morning. The risk is that iOS protects health data while the phone is locked, so I gave it two triggers: unplugging the phone in the morning, and a catch-up when the person opens an app they use every day. A native HealthKit app stays the upgrade path if Shortcuts proves unreliable.
- **An explainable score, not a black box.** Each reading is compared with the person's own recent nights (42 in the final numbers), and the score is a simple weighted sum the user can trace on a "Why today" screen. I chose this over anything learned or opaque because trust is the whole product: if people can't see why the app said "ease off", they won't act on it. It also defaults to being quiet, so a warning means something.
- **Email and password with no email at all.** I create each tester's account and reset passwords myself. It's free and needs no email provider. The cost is that a tester who forgets their password has to message me, which is fine for four people.
- **Privacy as a requirement, not a feature.** I'm holding other people's health data, so one person seeing another's data is the worst thing the app could do. Every table is locked to its owner at the database level, and an automated check fails the build if any table isn't.
- **Status on the lock screen.** The morning notification says "Ease off today: HRV well below your usual, sleep short." That's the most useful message without opening the app, but it's also visible on the lock screen. I accepted that for usefulness, made it part of the consent form, and show testers how to hide previews.
- **Convenience versus a clean security rule.** Vercel's official Supabase integration saves setup time, but it copies secret values into Vercel. I accepted it with a guardrail: every build is checked so no secret can ever reach the browser, and the check fails a deploy if one does.
- **Undoing a convenience once the data became real.** Before testers' health data arrived, I went back on a shortcut I'd accepted at the start: Vercel's Supabase integration had copied my full-access keys into Vercel, and Vercel's free plan lets it use stored content to train AI. I removed the integration, typed in the three public values by hand, replaced the secret key, reset the database password, and switched off the old-style keys. Checking each step turned up something I hadn't expected: the project already signed sign-ins with a new key, but still trusted the old signing secret, which was among the copies. Revoking it closed the last gap. Nobody was signed out, and a privacy check from the outside passed before and after.
- **A shared phone follows whoever signed in last.** If two people use the same phone, its notifications go to the person signed in now, so nobody ever sees someone else's status on their lock screen.
- **Changing the data format after measuring, not guessing.** My plan said the iPhone Shortcut would send one entry per reading. On my own phone that took 14 and a half minutes for one day of heart rate. Sending each column as a list (all the times, then all the values) took 1.3 seconds. I changed the format, and the server lines the columns back up into the same readings, so nothing downstream changed.
- **Moving a rule to where it can actually run.** I only want heart rate from 6pm to noon, the night that matters. It turned out the iPhone's Health search only works in whole days, so the Shortcut now fetches yesterday and today, and the server keeps 6pm to noon on arrival. Same rule, different place, same storage.
- **One bad reading shouldn't cost a month.** During my year's import, a single resting heart rate reading that ran past 24 hours made the server refuse a whole month. I changed the rule: a reading that fails a check is set aside and counted, and the rest is stored. A malformed post as a whole is still refused. Strict where it protects the data, forgiving where it would just lose it.
- **Deciding what counts as a night, then changing my mind with evidence.** The Watch records sleep as stages with gaps between them, so "last night" needed rules before any score could use it. I started with: stages up to 90 minutes apart are one sleep, a night needs at least 2 hours asleep, and sleeping heart rate needs at least 10 readings. Then I checked one real night against the Health app, and Clarivi showed about two hours less sleep. I had been awake for a long spell in the early morning and gone back to sleep, and my 90-minute rule threw the second part away. That happened on 1 night in 6. I switched to a rule anyone can predict: all sleep from 6pm to noon is that morning's night, and afternoon sleep is a nap. The numbers now match what testers will see in their own Health app, which matters for trust.
- **Making the score match the picture.** My design showed a sample morning: HRV 0.9 points, sleep 0.6, sleeping heart rate 0.1, total 1.6, "Ease off". Before building the score, I checked that a simple rule reproduces it exactly: each reading earns its weight times how far it is from my normal, measured in my own spread. It did, so the design, the explanation screen and the maths all tell the same story, and that sample day is now an automated test.
- **An illness check that only speaks.** When several overnight readings drift the wrong way together, Clarivi shows a short pattern note, never a diagnosis. I chose a rule that would have shown on about 1 night in 24 of my year, and kept it out of the score: it adds a note but never changes the status or the advice, so the score stays explainable.
- **Setting the numbers from my own year, and reporting a miss honestly.** Before any tester sees a status, the score's numbers had to be set from my year and frozen. Two changes were clearly right: gaps in my tracking left almost half my days stuck on "still learning your normal", so the normal now comes from the last 42 nights instead of 28, and moving "Ease off" from 1 point to 1.2 brought it down to about 1 day in 7.5, inside my "quiet by default" target. I left the weights alone, because there wasn't enough evidence to tune them without fitting noise. The other target, that at least 2 in 3 objectively disrupted days get flagged, failed: 1 of 13. The evidence was thin (no illness dates I could trust, and "easier workouts" may have been easier by choice), but I'd frozen the targets before building precisely so I couldn't explain a miss away. I recorded it as not met, and the testers' daily check-ins become the main evidence for the signal.
- **Changing how, not what.** The plan said the iPhone Shortcut would import my workouts for the signal check. A one-time export from the Health app did the same job in minutes, with no Shortcut changes and nothing for testers to run, so I took it and kept the export in a private folder.
- **Correct the past, but remember what people saw.** Clarivi recalculates recent days as more data arrives, so a morning's status can be corrected later, for example when the rest of a night syncs the next day. That's right for the analysis, but the test has to judge what each person was actually shown. So the morning's status, nudge and reason will be saved as shown, and the results will use that record.
- **Words built from rules, and reviewed before any screen used them.** The morning briefing isn't written by a model; it's put together from fixed sentence parts by rules: what was off, what it means, then what was normal. That keeps it explainable and lets tests hold every combination to the rules (no numbers, no condition names, at most three sentences). Before building the screens, I reviewed a sheet of every state's words on made-up days. My first change was the headline for a normal day: "You look well recovered" claimed something about my body that the data can't know, so it now says what the data shows: "Your readings are all normal". The same thinking changed a Ready day after a short night from "your body has recovered" to "A short night, but the rest looks normal".
- **Taking "normal" out of a health app.** When I read every sentence the app can show, one word kept bothering me: "normal". "Your sleeping heart rate was normal for you" sounds like a clinical verdict, and the opposite of normal sounds like something wrong. Clarivi only ever compares a reading with that person's own recent nights, so I replaced it everywhere: readings are "in", "above" or "below your usual range", and a new user is "Learning your usual levels". Because the consent text used the word once, it became version 2 and everyone agreed again, which was cheap before any tester had started. A test now fails if "normal" ever comes back on a screen. I kept "Ready" after weighing a dozen alternatives: it was clearer than any of them.
- **No dead ends while the app is half-built.** The designed card links to a weekly digest and a trends screen that don't exist until a later phase. Rather than links that go nowhere, I left them out until their screens exist, and the Settings button opens the account screen I already had as a stand-in.
- **Checking that my measure can see the failure.** The riskiest unknown was how often the iPhone blocks the morning sync because the phone is still locked. I planned to count it from the server's log, since on the first morning the blocked runs still contacted the server before stopping. The next morning a blocked run showed me an error and never reached the server at all: by then the Shortcut's first step read its "already synced today" file, and the lock blocks that too. Rather than rebuild the Shortcut mid-measurement, I kept the log, which still shows which automation delivered each morning, and added my own note on mornings I see the message. The backup automation caught up three minutes later, which is exactly why there are two. A few days in, the lock showed up a third way: a lock-screen prompt asking me to tap to carry on. When I ignored it, that run sent nothing; when I tapped Continue, it went through. The count so far says the morning charger run is blocked on most days, and every time a later run, the backup automation or the Sync now button still got that morning's readings in. Because testers will see the same prompt, the setup guide now tells them to unlock and tap Continue.
- **Sharing the Shortcut without sharing my key.** Testers install the same iPhone Shortcut I use, but mine holds my personal upload token, so sharing my own copy could have let anyone send readings into my account. I made the testers' link from a fresh blank copy, then downloaded what the link actually serves and checked it: no token, no name or email, and the install question that asks each tester for their own token. Only then did it go into the app.
- **Building to the strictest rules, not the nearest ones.** My testers may live in the Cayman Islands, Ontario, Quebec or Washington, DC, and Clarivi stores their health readings in the US. Before writing a word of the consent screen, I read the Cayman Data Protection Act and the rules in each place. They differ: Washington, DC has no health-data law in force yet, while Quebec asks for express consent, a written privacy assessment before data leaves the province, a named person in charge and a published privacy policy. Rather than tailor the app per tester, I chose to meet Quebec's rules for everyone, so one design is safe wherever someone lives. I also decided against a paid legal review for a small test with people I know, and wrote down that this changes before strangers are invited.
- **Fixing a mistake while it was cheap.** My database project was created in the wrong region at first. It was still empty, so I recreated it in the right region the same day, before anything depended on it.

## Major bugs and how I fixed them

- **Switching off sign-ups switched off sign-in.** I wanted nobody to be able to create an account except me. The obvious setting turned off email sign-ups, but it also turned off email sign-in entirely, so nobody could log in. We caught it while testing on the local copy, before it reached the live project, used the general "no new sign-ups" switch instead, and recorded the trap so it wouldn't come back.
- **Setting a new password signed people out.** On first sign-in, testers must replace their temporary password. Saving the new password ended every existing session for security, which dropped people back at the sign-in screen. Now the app signs straight back in with the new password, so it feels seamless.
- **A security check that cried wolf.** The first version of the "no secrets in the build" check flagged the database library itself, because the library contains the text it uses to recognise secret keys. I rewrote the check to look for real keys and the exact secret values from the hosting settings, and proved it works by planting a fake secret and watching the build fail.
- **A sync that sent nothing.** My first real run from the phone reached the server and was accepted, but carried zero readings, even though my Health app was full of them. Rather than guess, I had Claude build a small "check" Shortcut that sends nothing and just shows on screen what the phone finds. Four quick rounds on my phone found two problems: iOS reads a plain date like "2026-10-03" as noon, not midnight, and the Health search ignores times of day altogether. Those findings drove the two decisions above.
- **Importing a year through an iPhone Shortcut.** The plan was simple: send a year of history, one month per post. My phone said no, five different ways, and I found each one with a tiny "check" Shortcut that showed on screen what was happening instead of guessing. iOS ignored date steps of whole months, so I step in hours. It stopped when one step handled about 4,000 heart rate readings, so heart rate is read a day at a time. It timed out posts bigger than about 400 KB, so each month goes in small parts. And it failed any post made straight after a loop, so the last part is sent inside it. The result: a year of my data (about 126,000 readings) in about 15 minutes, and if it ever stops, running it again carries on where it left off.
- **A recalculation that got slower the more history it had.** Turning a month of readings into nights took a fraction of a second, but a full year took 23 seconds, longer than the database allows. The query was correct; the database had simply chosen to redo the whole heart rate count once for every night. I rewrote it so each night looks up only its own readings, and a made-up year now takes about 0.2 seconds. It came back once more in a different step, at 110 seconds, right after a big import, when the database hadn't yet counted its new rows; that step now works from a small table of its own. The lesson: test with a year of data, not a week, and straight after a bulk load, before real users arrive.
- **Sleep counted twice.** The first time my real nights were built, most looked right, but one night showed nearly twice as much time asleep as the length of the sleep itself. Two records covered the same time: a coarse second set of sleep stages on top of the Watch's own, and on some July nights the same stretch logged as both asleep and awake. The plan had assumed only the Watch writes sleep stages, and the iPhone gives no source name to check. It affected 18 of 239 nights. I chose to count each minute once and, when records disagree, trust "awake", which is closest to the Watch's own detailed record. Looking at real data before building the score on top of it caught this; the made-up test data never would have.
- **The independent check earned its keep on its first run.** I had a second program recalculate my whole year from the raw readings, written from the rules rather than copied from the database code. All 237 statuses matched, but one night's resting heart rate didn't, and digging in showed my data came from two Apple Watches and an app. The spec said "Watch readings only", and nothing enforced it. Now Clarivi recognises a watch as anything that records heart rate, and when two watches disagree it takes the middle value. Without the check, I'd never have known.
- **"Empty" means "unknown", not "no".** Twice the same trap appeared. An optional setting (cap two-reading days at Ease off) was switched off by leaving it empty, and an empty value quietly made Rest impossible on those days. Later, a safety check meant to require every reading's minimum spread let a missing value through, because the database treats "unknown" as passing. Both were caught by tests written to expect the failure, which is why I write those tests first.
- **A password prompt that wasn't hidden.** The check asks for my password and is meant to hide it, but the terminal inside the app I was using showed it anyway. I treated it as exposed: the scripts now remember a sign-in so the password is typed once, in a terminal that hides it, and I changed my password.
- **My own health readings in a public repository.** The end-of-phase code review found that, while writing up decisions, I had quoted some of my actual readings (a couple of heart rates, sleep times, a trip's dates) in the project docs, which are public. The rule was already written down; I just hadn't checked against it. I removed them, rewrote the unmerged branches so the old commits were gone, and the docs now describe what happened without the numbers. It's the reason the review step exists.
- **Tests that passed on some runs and failed on others.** The new screen tests sign in a made-up tester and answer every database request with made-up data. Some runs failed at random, saving a check-in with a real "unauthorised" error. The cause: a moment after the page opened, the app's service worker took over its network requests, and those went past the test's made-up answers to the real local server. Blocking the service worker in these tests made them pass every time across repeated runs. A flaky test is worse than none, so I didn't move on until they were steady.
- **A lost phone would have kept showing my status.** Testing "Sign out everywhere" on my phone, I noticed it ended every sign-in but left every device registered for notifications, so a lost or shared phone would still have shown my status on its lock screen each morning. Now it stops notifications to all my devices first, and an app whose sign-in was ended elsewhere goes straight to the sign-in screen the next time it opens. The manual check found what the automated ones didn't, because the tests checked the sign-in, not the lock screen. After the fix I ran the check again, signed in on my iPhone and a laptop browser: one tap on the phone sent both to the sign-in screen, and that was the last of Phase 5's checks.
- **My own email in a public repository.** The security review before testers join searched every version of every file for keys, passwords and personal details. The files were clean, but the commit records weren't: each time I clicked "Merge pull request" on GitHub, it stamped the merge with my personal email, because my account's email privacy setting was off. I turned the setting on and chose to rewrite the six merges to GitHub's private address, checking that every commit's files stayed identical. The lesson was that privacy lives in metadata too, and that a rewrite has limits: GitHub keeps its own copies inside the merge requests, which only its support team can remove.
- **A code update stopped halfway by my cloud storage.** Updating my Mac's copy of the code failed partway, twice, with a timeout, leaving some files half-written. The cause was my Documents folder syncing to iCloud: macOS had kept some project files only in the cloud, and the update gave up waiting for them. Setting the folder to stay downloaded fixed it, and nothing was lost, because everything was already on GitHub. It also changed a decision: backups of testers' data now live in an encrypted disk image outside Documents, so they can never drift into iCloud.
- **Previews that didn't know where the database was.** Vercel's integration only filled in settings for production, so preview builds of work in progress showed "isn't set up yet." I added the public values for previews by hand and documented them.
- **New words on screen, old words in the notification.** After my wording review I released the new words at 1am, and the app showed them. The next morning, the notification on my lock screen still said my readings were "close to your normal", the exact word I'd just removed. The app and the server programs that write notifications each carry their own copy of the wording, and the release had only updated the app. Re-sending the two server programs fixed it in a minute, and I added a release rule: whenever the shared wording changes, the server programs go out too. My real-life test caught what the automated tests couldn't, because they check each copy on its own, not which copy is live.
- **A sync that said "done" too early.** One morning my Watch recorded my sleep as plain "asleep", without the usual stages. Clarivi only builds a night from the Watch's stages, so it rightly gave no status, but the quick check that tells the Shortcut "last night is in, you can stop" counted the plain record. So every later sync that day stopped early: if the stages had arrived later, Clarivi would never have seen them. Two parts of the server were using two different rules for the same question. I chose to make the quick check follow the analysis's rule, rather than loosen the analysis just before the test, and I put a review of stage-less nights on the list for after the test.
- **The warning I built caught my own mistake.** To get the new app icon, I removed Clarivi from my Home Screen and added it back. That quietly created a fresh app, and a fresh app has no notification sign-up, so the next morning my notification would never have arrived. The card told me first: "Notifications are off on this phone", the warning I'd built for exactly this, and two taps in Settings fixed it. Testers get the same warning, and the setup checklist now mentions it.
- **A library update that broke the build overnight.** A merge request's checks failed on code that hadn't changed. My server programs asked for "any version 2" of the database library, and a new version released that morning was missing a part. I pinned the last version that passed, so the library only changes when I choose to, with the tests. The checks caught it before it could reach a deploy.

## Significant improvements

- **Proving the riskiest part first.** Before building any screens, I ran a spike on my own iPhone: install the app, sign in, receive a notification and tap it. A test notification arrived through Apple's push service about 15 seconds after I asked for it, and tapping it opened the app still signed in. The notification library worked first time, so I didn't need the fallback.
- **Guardrails that run on every change.** Every push to GitHub runs the app tests, the database tests, the secret check, and end-to-end tests that open the app in Safari's engine at iPhone size. I deliberately added an unprotected table on a scratch branch to prove the safety check really fails the build.
- **A plan I can keep current.** I keep five living documents (product spec, design, tech spec, progress and the MVP scope) and update them after each phase, so decisions and the reasons behind them never get lost. I also converted my original MVP PDF into an editable document so it could keep up with reality. To make them easy to read, a small script turns the five files into one site, with the plan's ticks stored on the site and a searchable list of every decision, and it rebuilds in one command whenever the files change. Each task keeps its tick even when tasks are added or reworded, because ids are matched by the task's words, not its position.
- **Checking the risk before building on it.** Heart rate variability carries the most weight in the morning score, and the plan flagged that it might be too sparse at night. Once my year was in, I counted: 239 of 240 tracked nights have at least one HRV reading during sleep, with a median of three. The risk was real on paper and absent in my data, and now I know rather than hope.
- **A Shortcut generated from code.** Building an iPhone Shortcut by hand means dozens of taps that are easy to get wrong. Instead the Shortcut is generated by a script, checked automatically on every push (every step wired up, valid data, no secrets inside), and signed on my Mac so it installs with one tap. The signed file stays private because Apple's signature includes my account email.
- **A code review at the end of every phase, and it pays off.** Each phase closes with a review of everything it changed, the findings fixed and the tests re-run before I call it done. Phase 1's found ten things; the two that mattered most were a gateway error that could have been read as success and quietly marked a month of my import as done, and import progress that could say "12 of 12" while a month was still half-sent. Phase 2's found fourteen, the most important being my own readings in the public docs (above), plus a morning sync before the night arrived being labelled "not enough data" instead of "sleep still in progress", and failed recalculations that were never retried. Anything needing a judgement came to me: I accepted a small loss of past trip heart rate, kept the simple 6pm rule despite one rare edge case, and moved "remember what people saw" into the notifications phase.
- **An independent check that runs on every change.** The second program that recalculates my year also runs on every push against a deliberately messy made-up history: long awake spells, a second watch, an app's readings, naps, a trip eight hours ahead, missing readings, an unfinished night. To prove it isn't a check that always passes, I changed one number in it on purpose and watched it fail.
- **The morning screens, tested at iPhone size against the design.** The check-in, the readiness card in every state and Why today are tested in Safari's engine at iPhone size, with the clock set to the design's sample morning and made-up readings, so the sample day must come out exactly as designed. One test checks that the morning card fits on one screen of a 390-point iPhone, including the longest briefing, and another that no screen ever shows the score's weights.
- **Asking "did you follow it?" at the right moment.** The test only shows the nudge has value if people say whether they followed it, so the question comes at 8pm, when the day is done, only on days the nudge asked for a change, with Yes and No made exactly equal so the design never leans on the answer. If it goes unanswered, the next morning asks about yesterday first, before today's check-in and status, so neither answer is shaped by what the app says next. And the question is about the nudge each person actually saw that morning, which is saved, not a recalculated one.
- **Testing on myself before anyone else.** Before any screens exist, I read my own status each morning for two to three weeks and note how I actually felt. On day one it said "Ready" after a very short night when I felt too tired to train. That's exactly the weakness my year's numbers hinted at. I logged it instead of retuning on the spot: one morning is an anecdote, and a pattern over the self-test is evidence I can act on before testers start.
- **Real mornings as the test script.** Some checks can only be done on a real morning, so I planned them like experiments. For the 11:30 reminder, I switched both automations off the night before: the reminder arrived with no health detail, Sync now worked, and the card showed today's status marked Late, exactly as designed. Each real morning also turned up something the guide needed. The first Sync now triggers an iPhone question ("Allow ... to output 1 text item?"), and a locked phone can leave a "Tap to run" prompt. Both now have a line in the setup guide, so testers aren't surprised by what surprised me.
