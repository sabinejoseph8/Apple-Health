# Clarivi MVP

Scoping the smallest version that proves the idea | Apple Health Analytics Tool | Sabine Joseph

**Format note:** converted from MVP.pdf on 2 October 2026. This Markdown file is now the version that is kept up to date; a PDF can be made from it when one is needed.

## How to Use This Document

This is a thinking aid, not a decision. Sections 2 to 4 give a proposed starting position for the MVP boundary, written so you can accept, cut or move each item. Section 5 lists what should be investigated before building. Section 6 lists the decisions only you can make, and Section 7 the questions whose answers would change the scope. Nothing here is final until you say so.

## 1. The Core Value the MVP Must Prove

**Core value in one sentence:** Apple Watch records the numbers; this tool explains them. Using only their own Apple Watch data, a person sees in plain words what last night's readings mean against their own normal, why today's status was set, and one action for today, which Apple's own features do not explain.

An MVP earns its place by testing the three hypotheses this value rests on:

- **Feasibility:** data can flow from the watch to the tool reliably enough to support a daily signal.
- **Signal:** baseline deviation detection flags the days that actually felt off and stays quiet on normal days, often enough to be trusted.
- **Value:** the user reads the card and the nudge changes a decision at least some of the time.

Anything that is not needed to test one of these three is a candidate for cutting from the MVP.

### Success criteria (D9): what makes the MVP done

A four-week tester period with all four users, judged as a mix: hard gates for Feasibility, target ranges plus end-of-test conversations for Signal and Value. The targets below are confirmed (29 September 2026) and must not change once results start arriving.

- **Gate, sync:** each user has a successful morning sync on at least 26 of 28 days.
- **Gate, push:** a notification is delivered on at least 90% of synced days that have a status (D36).
- **Gate, isolation:** no user ever sees another user's data, and the cross-user access test passes. Absolute, no tolerance.
- **Gate, correctness:** the SQL matches the pandas reference on your backfilled year, stage by stage, and no unfinished night is ever scored.
- **Signal target, your year:** of the disrupted days in your backfilled year (see below), at least 2 in 3 would have been called ease off or rest. No one is asked to remember how they felt; the check uses only evidence already in the data or in your calendar.
- **Evidence for your year:** a list of objectively disrupted days, built before testers start: travel days, found automatically from timezone changes in the samples; illness and major events, entered from your calendar into the event table; and workouts far below your usual duration or intensity for that activity. It only catches off days that left a trace, so a day that felt bad but changed nothing is missed; the daily check-ins during the test cover that gap.
- **Signal target, quiet by default:** ease off and rest together fire on no more than about 1 day in 7, on your year and during the test.
- **Signal target, testers:** the daily check-in ("off") and the tool's status agree more often than chance, reported per user over days that have a status (D36); with four people this shows direction, not proof. Check-ins can be skipped (D33), so the completion rate per tester is reported alongside.
- **Value target, engagement:** each tester taps the notification or opens the card on at least 5 days a week.
- **Value target, action:** on days the nudge says anything other than train as planned, testers mark it followed about half the time or more.
- **Value target, retention:** all three testers are still syncing and opening the card in week four.
- **Conversations:** a short conversation with each tester at the end: did the tool change a decision, what did they ignore and why, would they keep using it. Their answers carry as much weight as the percentages.

## 2. Proposed Must-Haves (the thin slice)

The smallest end-to-end path: ingest, clean, compute, show, nudge. Each item states why it is in, so you can challenge the reasoning rather than the list.

- **Data ingestion via automated sync:** each user installs a Shortcut on their iPhone that reads their Health data and posts it to an authenticated upload endpoint on the site. Run once, it backfills the last year of history in monthly chunks (Decision D26); run each morning by two automations (Decision D16): one when the phone is unplugged from its charger, and a catch-up when the tester first opens an everyday app of their choice, it posts the previous night and day. There is no manual export in the MVP (Decision D2). Each post is JSON carrying raw samples, not daily summaries (Decision D18): every reading with its timestamp, timezone offset, source device and unit, plus a schema version, a backfill flag and a month ID; the user is identified by their upload token, never by the body. Resting HR, HRV, sleep stages and respiratory rate are sent in full; heart rate is sent only for a fixed, generous overnight window (6pm to noon), which the server trims to the actual sleep window. The endpoint is idempotent, so a Shortcut that runs twice or is re-run by hand never duplicates data. For your account only, the backfill also fetches workout summaries (activity type, date, duration and average heart rate), used solely to build the disrupted-day evidence for the Signal check (D9). Wrist temperature is not collected in the MVP. The source stays pluggable, so a HealthKit app or bridge app can replace Shortcuts later without touching anything downstream.
- **Two morning triggers:** the same Shortcut runs from two personal automations (Decision D16). The wake trigger fires when the phone is unplugged from its charger, which for anyone who charges overnight is a reliable sign they are up; it needs no iPhone alarm. Unplugging does not unlock the phone, so this run may still be blocked. The catch-up trigger fires when the tester opens an everyday app they choose during onboarding, which guarantees the phone is unlocked. Before doing anything, the Shortcut checks the time (it acts only between 4am and noon) and whether today's sync already succeeded, and stops if so; a midday or evening unplug therefore does nothing. The server then decides whether the night is actually over (R24), so whichever run first finds a finished night produces the score and the morning push; any later run changes nothing.
- **Per-user upload tokens:** each user's Shortcut authenticates with its own upload token, generated in the app. A token can write that user's data and nothing else, can never read, and can be revoked from the app. Because the token travels inside the configured Shortcut, a configured Shortcut must never be shared (Risk R19).
- **Cleaning and a daily feature table:** explicit day-boundary and timezone rules, coverage per day, and three simplifying rules that keep the cleaning in plain SQL (Decision D10). One source: every metric is taken from the Apple Watch only, since every user wears it overnight and the iPhone does not measure heart rate, HRV or respiratory rate, which removes most overlapping-source handling. One sleep window: each night's window runs from the first to the last "asleep" sample in the Watch's own sleep stages, and overnight heart rate is trimmed to it with a simple join. One definition: sleeping HR is the median heart rate during asleep periods. Because the Shortcut sends raw samples (D18), all of this happens on the server; the database keeps the raw samples and builds the clean daily table from them, so a rule change is a recompute, never a re-sync. Any step that still proves awkward in SQL moves to a Supabase Edge Function in the same project.
- **Minimal data quality gate:** a per-day confidence label; low-coverage days are excluded from baselines and marked on the card. Trust in the signal depends on it.
- **Baseline drift and anomaly detection on three metrics:** sleeping HR, overnight HRV and sleep duration (Decision D5). Each metric is compared with the user's own baseline: the median of the previous 28 days, never including the day being judged, with a flag when a reading sits more than 2 scaled MADs from it. A scaled MAD is the median absolute deviation multiplied by 1.4826, so that it matches a standard deviation while still ignoring the odd extreme night. No flag is raised unless the window holds at least 21 valid days, so a stretch of missed nights suppresses flags rather than producing unreliable ones; while a baseline is still building, D36 decides what the card shows. The same fixed defaults apply to everyone (Decision D11). With scaled MAD, per-metric flags are uncommon (roughly 1 day in 45 per metric in one direction, if readings are bell-shaped), so a flag means something; the readiness thresholds (D24) still decide what the nudge says. Written as SQL (window functions and percentile_cont) and run each time new data arrives for a user (Decision D19), filling the insights table. Note that D5 scopes the score, not what is ingested: the illness composite below needs two extra daily values that the Shortcut can carry at almost no cost.
- **Readiness score:** rule-based with fixed weights (Decision D6): each of the three metrics is expressed as a deviation from the user's own 28-day baseline, the deviations are combined with fixed weights, and two thresholds separate ready, ease off and rest. Fully expressible in SQL and fully explainable: the card can show the arithmetic. The initial weights and thresholds, and whether the card shows how the status was decided (the Why today screen) or just the result, are Decision D24. Parameters are stored, not hard-coded, so user-tunable or learned weights can follow later without a rewrite. A night with a reading missing is scored from the other two (D35).
- **Today's readiness card:** status (ready, ease off, rest), the two or three metrics driving it in plain language, the nudge with its one-sentence reason, and the data freshness, laid out to fill the first screen of an iPhone (Decision D27). The full arithmetic sits one tap away on the Why today screen (Decision D24), titled with the day's status, for example "Why ease off today". Each reading is shown against the tester's normal range in plain words, with its numbers (normal range, difference from normal, and how it compares with the last 4 weeks) on tap. A closing section explains how the status is decided, with each reading's points, the day's total and the ease off and rest thresholds on tap; the weights themselves are not shown, only the order in which the readings matter. Opens of that screen are logged, so you learn whether testers check how the status was decided.
- **Nudge:** one action from a fixed vocabulary (train as planned, train easy, rest, prioritise sleep). Sent each morning as a push notification to the tester's iPhone and shown on the card (Decision D4); tapping the notification opens the card in the home-screen app. The notification carries the status and the reason, for example "Ease off today: HRV well below your usual, sleep short" (Decision D29). Computed as soon as the morning sync lands (Decision D19) and stating its freshness plainly ("as of this morning's sync"). Daily cadence is the target (Decision D12), which makes the sync spike in Section 5 the gating item (A7).
- **Minimal trend view:** for each metric in the score, one line with the baseline band and flagged points, sized for a narrow iPhone screen first (D27). This is the detailed dashboard reduced to the minimum that lets a user verify the card.
- **Accounts, login and data isolation:** each user signs in with an email address and password (Decision D30), using Supabase's built-in login, and sees only their own data. Sign-up is closed to the public: you create each tester's account yourself in the Supabase dashboard, so nobody else can create one, and the MVP sends no email at all (no invitations, confirmations or reset emails). Each tester gets a temporary password from you privately and changes it once in the app. On the iPhone, iCloud Keychain saves the password and fills it with Face ID, so day to day it feels close to Face ID sign-in. Row-level isolation between users is a correctness requirement, not a feature: one user seeing another's health data is the worst failure this MVP can have (Risk R13). Because testers use the tool from an iPhone every morning, sessions are long-lived, balanced by a sign-out-everywhere control and re-entering the password before sensitive actions (reissuing an upload token, deleting data); a 30-day idle expiry follows once the project moves to Pro after the test, since it needs the paid plan (D37). Includes an in-app change-password screen and a minimum password length; a forgotten password is reset by you, setting a new temporary password and telling the tester privately. Nothing fancier.
- **Installable iPhone web app:** the site is mobile-first and installable to the iPhone home screen as a web app (Decision D27): an app icon, full-screen display, and no App Store. Laptop use still works but is the adapted layout, not the primary one: the same single column as on the iPhone, centred, at most 480 points wide (D39).
- **Push notifications:** iOS delivers web push only to web apps added to the home screen, and only after the user grants permission from a tap inside the app, so the onboarding flow asks for it once, after install. The server stores one push subscription per device, sends the nudge once the analysis triggered by that user's upload has finished (D19), and logs whether each notification was delivered and tapped. At most one morning nudge per user per day: if the Shortcut runs twice, or a tester re-runs it by hand, the second analysis updates the card but does not send a second nudge. On days the nudge asked for a change (train easy, rest, prioritise sleep), an evening follow-up push goes out at 8pm local time asking whether they followed it (Decision D32); it is not sent on train-as-planned days or when no morning nudge went out. Its wording carries no health detail, since it also shows on the lock screen (R23). The server works out each tester's local 8pm from the timezone offsets in their latest samples. If no sync has succeeded by 11:30am local time (Decision D34), a reminder push goes out: "No sync yet this morning. Run your readiness Shortcut before noon to get today's nudge." It carries no health detail and is sent at most once that day. A sync that lands between 11:30am and noon, whether by hand or by a late automation, still produces today's card and nudge, marked as late, and the 8pm follow-up still applies on change days. Only if nothing syncs by noon does the day go without a nudge (R1), because the Shortcut's window stops runs after noon.
- **Analysis on upload:** each accepted upload triggers that user's analysis (baselines, score, composite, nudge) for the affected dates, and then the push (Decision D19). The weekly digest remains a scheduled job. Backfill chunks must not each trigger a full recompute and a notification: backfill uploads are marked as such, recomputed once when the backfill completes, and never send a push.
- **Usage and outcome logging:** server-side record of logins, card views, flags shown, nudges sent and tapped, and whether the user marked the nudge followed, including when and from where (the evening push, the card after 8pm, or the next morning's card). Because everything runs on your server, this replaces any returned file and is your direct evidence for the Signal and Value hypotheses. It settles D8: the feedback button is in.
- **Evening follow-up:** on days the nudge asked for a change, the question "Did you follow today's nudge?" appears only from 8pm local time (D32). At 8pm a push asks it, and from then the card shows it with yes and no above the morning's briefing; tapping the push opens the card on the answer. Before 8pm the card shows the nudge without the question. The answer can be changed until the next morning. If the tester still has not answered by the next morning, that morning's card asks about yesterday before showing today's status. Train-as-planned days are never asked, which matches the D9 action target.
- **Daily check-in:** one tap on the card each day: how do you feel today, good, okay or off. It is shown before the status is revealed when the card opens, stored alongside the day's score, and never feeds the score, so it stays an independent measure of how the day actually felt (D9). Testers can skip it (D33): the card then appears straight away and keeps a small prompt so they can answer later that day. An answer can be changed until the next morning's check-in appears. Every answer is logged with its time and whether the tester had already seen the status, and the Signal check uses the first answer given before the status was seen wherever one exists. Because the notification already shows the status and reason on the lock screen (D29), a tester may see the verdict before answering; that bias is noted in the results rather than designed away.
- **Consent and security:** written consent from each tester; HTTPS everywhere; encryption at rest for the database and all synced data; per-user upload tokens that can write but never read (R19); access to production limited to you; a retention rule and deletion on request; no third-party analytics scripts on pages that show health data. You are now holding other people's health data on a server you operate, so this is a must-have (Risk R8, back at full strength).
- **Tester onboarding:** a one-page guide covering adding the web app to the home screen, signing in from inside that home-screen app (not from Safari) with the temporary password you give them, changing it in the app and saving the new one to iCloud Keychain, allowing notifications, installing the Shortcut and adding their upload token, running the one-time backfill, setting up the two morning automations and choosing their catch-up app, and what to do if a morning run fails (run the Shortcut by hand), plus what the card means, how to mark a nudge followed, and contacting you directly if they forget their password.

### Decided in (D8): three items that were borderline

- **Illness and strain composite:** in, confirmed. A multi-signal flag that fires only when several overnight markers move together in the same direction. The score stays at three metrics; the composite additionally reads Apple's resting HR (one daytime value Apple publishes at the end of each day, so a morning composite uses yesterday's) and respiratory rate where a tester's watch records them, and degrades gracefully when they are absent, stating which inputs it had. Wrist temperature is excluded from the MVP entirely and can be added to the composite later as one more optional input. This fixes the extra columns in the daily file (see Data ingestion). Brings R7 (health-adjacent wording) into the must-do list: the composite is a pattern note, never a diagnosis.
- **Weekly digest:** in. A plain-language summary each week of what moved against baseline, what was flagged, how many days had data and how many nudges were followed, shown on a page in the app. Written from a fixed template filled from the insights table for the MVP (Decision D22); a model-written version, constrained to the same numbers and checked against a list of forbidden claims, is a later feature the architecture should allow for. Shown as an in-app page only (Decision D23).
- **Nudge feedback capture:** decided in, as part of usage and outcome logging above.

## 3. Proposed Out of Scope for the MVP

Out of scope means not built for the MVP, not abandoned. Grouped by the reason for exclusion:

### Needs more history than an MVP will have

- Lagged cross-metric relationships (60 to 90 days), personal sleep need estimation, seasonality and periodic decomposition, changepoint detection, and the causal layer (guided self-experiments and natural experiment analysis). Decided: the causal layer is out of the MVP and is presented as method and design, with its schemas kept in the architecture (Section 4, Decision D7).

### Needs a workout-level data model the core signal does not require

- Strength session analysis, heart rate recovery, intensity distribution across cardio, standardized fitness test tracking.

### Valuable but not on the core value path

- Weight and body composition trend, mobility and gait metrics, sedentary patterns, behavioural and adherence patterns, nudge calibration loop, the full progressive-disclosure dashboard, and the event annotation user interface (the schema stays in, see Section 4).

## 4. Future Features the Architecture Should Support Now

These are cheap to design in and expensive to retrofit. None of them add MVP features; they keep the door open.

- **Keep raw samples, not just daily aggregates:** the MVP stores every sample the Shortcut sends (D18) and builds the daily table from it. Heart rate recovery, strength analysis and intensity distribution will also need daytime heart rate, which the MVP does not fetch; adding them means widening the Shortcut's heart-rate window and extending the backfill, with nothing already stored redone.
- **A module contract:** every analysis module reads the daily feature table and emits insights in one standard shape (metric, date, value, baseline, deviation, severity, explanation) into an insights table. The card, digest and nudge only ever read that table, so new modules plug in without touching the interface. This also lets the analysis live wherever is convenient, a database function today or a Python service tomorrow (D10 option B), without the app noticing.
- **Event annotation schema:** a table of dated events with a type and a note. The MVP uses it straight away: you enter illness and major events from your calendar for your backfilled year, and travel days are added automatically, to form the disrupted-day evidence for the Signal check (D9). No interface is needed yet; entries can go in directly or from a small file. The causal and correlation modules will depend on the same table later.
- **Experiment registry schema:** hypothesis, outcome metric, baseline and test windows, status, timestamps. Pre-registration is only credible if the record exists before the result. With the causal layer out of the MVP, this schema is what lets the design be shown as ready to run.
- **Nudge log with feedback fields:** date, nudge, reason, followed (yes/no), next-day outcome. Enables calibration later and doubles as evidence for the Value hypothesis.
- **Workout and segment tables:** workout id, type, start and end, lap markers, linked HR series. Populated by the parser now, consumed later.
- **Delivery channel abstraction:** card, digest and nudge as content objects with pluggable channels (in-app, web push, email), so changing how things arrive does not mean rewriting them. The MVP uses in-app and web push for the nudge (D4) and in-app only for the digest (D23).
- **Digest writer abstraction:** the digest job separates computing the week's facts from writing the sentences, so the template writer can be replaced by a guarded model-written one (D22) without touching the numbers.
- **Sync-path abstraction:** ingestion as a pluggable source (a Shortcuts automation now; a HealthKit app or bridge app later) with the same parsed output, so switching sync path is a new adapter, not a rewrite.
- **User scoping done properly:** multi-user is now a must-have (Section 2), so this is about doing it well: user id on every table, isolation enforced in one place (a query layer or row-level security), and an automated test that tries to read another user's data and fails.
- **Deployment discipline:** a single deployed version means no version drift across testers, but changes now affect everyone at once. Keep a changelog, tag releases, and freeze score logic during a test period so evidence stays comparable. Every change first goes to a Vercel preview address (D21) and is checked on your own phone before it goes live.
- **Configurable parameters:** baseline windows, thresholds and score weights stored as parameters, not constants.

## 5. Investigate Before Building

### Product assumptions to test

- **A1** Users will act on a daily nudge. You can test this on yourself for two to three weeks with a hand-computed nudge from your own data, before any interface exists.
- **A2** A readiness signal is trusted without external validation. Check flagged days in your backfilled year against objectively disrupted days (travel, calendar illness and events, and workouts far below your usual), not against memory; during the test, the daily check-ins provide the in-the-moment comparison.
- **A3** Target users wear the watch overnight. HRV, sleeping HR and respiratory rate all depend on it. Confirmed for you and all three testers.
- **A4** Retired for the MVP: wrist temperature is not collected for the score or the composite. It remains relevant only if a later version adds it as an optional composite input (Series 8, Ultra and later only).
- **A5** Retired: no second app is needed. Testers' iPhones run the Shortcuts automation for both the backfill and the daily sync (D2), which they have confirmed they can do.
- **A6** At least 28 days of history exists before the tool is useful. Met for you with five years of history; confirm each tester has at least 28 days, which the backfill then covers on day one.
- **A7** The Shortcuts automation runs reliably every day on testers' phones without them doing anything. Unproven: iOS protects Health data while the phone is locked, so locked-phone behaviour and background limits (R9) are the main unknowns. Test on your phone and one tester's in week one, with both triggers (D16).
- **A11** Every user charges their iPhone overnight, so unplugging it in the morning marks waking. Confirm this at onboarding. For anyone who charges during the day instead, the morning unplug never happens and the charger trigger becomes just a second catch-up; for them, swap the wake trigger for Sleep Focus turning off or a fixed early time, with the catch-up trigger unchanged.
- **A8** Resolved: you wear an Apple Watch Ultra (1st generation), and testers wear Ultra (1st generation) and SE (2nd generation), all overnight; all five MVP metrics are recorded on both models. Neither model can run watchOS 27, so Apple's new Readiness score, Recovery HRV and five-second heart rate (Series 12 and Ultra 4 only) are on no user's wrist, and your backfilled year is in the same data format throughout. Every user, you included, keeps the same watch through the test, since a new model would restart that person's baselines. The SE 2's lack of wrist temperature no longer matters now that temperature is out of the MVP. Absolute HRV readings can differ between models; that is harmless because every baseline is personal, but testers should never be compared to each other on raw values. The score must still cope with an occasional missing night.
- **A9** Testers will sign in to the account you create for them, install a Shortcut on their iPhone, and log in daily from any device. The automation posts to your upload endpoint each morning; if a run fails they can run the same Shortcut by hand. Confirmed: testers have iPhones that run automations and have agreed to server-side storage. Daily login remains to be observed.
- **A10** SQL can carry the MVP analysis. Baseline median and MAD, rolling windows and a weighted score are expressible in Postgres, and the D10 simplifying rules (Watch-only sources, Watch sleep stages as the sleep window, sleeping HR as a median) keep the cleaning within plain SQL, with Supabase Edge Functions for anything that is not. With the Shortcut posting raw samples as JSON (D2, D18), no export parsing is needed at all. Later modules such as seasonality decomposition or the causal layer will likely exceed SQL and be the point at which a Python service is added through the module contract.

### The chosen sync path: iPhone Shortcuts (spike first)

The iOS Shortcuts app has a "Find Health Samples" action that can pull readings for selected types over a date range, and a "Get Contents of URL" action that can post them to a web address as JSON. The same Shortcut does two jobs: run once, it backfills the last year of history, month by month (D26); run by two morning automations (the phone being unplugged from its charger, and a catch-up when an everyday app is opened; D16), it posts the previous night and day. The unknowns to settle in a half-day spike on your phone and one tester's: whether a scheduled run can read Health data when the phone is locked (iOS protects it while locked); how often the unplug run finds the phone locked and has to wait for the catch-up, and how long after waking the nudge typically arrives; how long a one-year backfill takes and whether it completes reliably (R20); and how large a month of raw samples is in practice, dominated by overnight heart rate (D18). A rough estimate is a couple of hundred heart-rate readings a night, about 70,000 a year, or a few hundred kilobytes per monthly post. If the spike fails, the upgrade path is a HealthKit companion app, not a return to manual exports.

### Dependencies

- **Sync path:** an iPhone Shortcut on each user's phone (D2). No app to build or install; depends on Apple's Shortcuts Health actions and automation behaviour, which can change with iOS updates. A HealthKit companion app remains the upgrade path if Shortcuts proves unreliable (Xcode, Swift, an Apple Developer Program membership at $99 a year, and TestFlight).
- **Email delivery:** none in the MVP (D30). You create each tester's account in the Supabase dashboard and reset forgotten passwords yourself. Supabase's built-in email sender only reaches members of your own Supabase team, at 2 messages an hour, so it could not send tester invitations anyway; a wider rollout would need an email provider connected to Supabase and a domain you own.
- **Hosting and operations:** Vercel serves the front end (the React code, icon and service worker) from your repository, and Supabase hosts the database, login, storage, upload endpoint and push sending (D17, D21). Health data never passes through Vercel: the phone talks to Supabase directly. Both stay on free plans through the build and the four-week test, and Supabase moves to Pro after the test (D37). On Supabase, encryption at rest is on every plan; the free plan has no automatic backups, so you make your own with the Supabase CLI's db dump command, saved to an encrypted folder on your laptop at least weekly and before every database change (R8). There is one Supabase project, with a local copy on your laptop for building and testing database changes (D38). Vercel's free plan covers personal, non-commercial use, which fits a capstone. Someone (you) is on the hook when either is down.
- **Notification channel:** web push to the home-screen web app (D4, D27). Needs a key pair for signing notifications (VAPID), a table of push subscriptions, and a small sending function on Supabase, all standard for web push. No third-party notification service is required.
- **Test data:** your five years of history plus three testers' histories, with consent to server-side storage confirmed (Risk R8).
- **Class timeline:** no fixed due date for the MVP or the final demo, and about 40 hours a week available, and no self-set milestone (D25). Time is not the constraint; scope is. With neither a date nor a milestone, the success criteria (D9) define when the MVP is done: a four-week tester period judged against the gates and targets in Section 1.

### Technical risks

| Risk | Why it matters | How to de-risk |
|---|---|---|
| R1 Stale data when a sync is missed | A missed or failed automation run means the nudge is computed on old data, which can be wrong for today, and a wrong nudge costs more trust than no nudge. | Show freshness on the card and each user's last sync time. If no sync has succeeded by 11:30am local time (D34), send one reminder push and show the missed sync on the card; a sync before noon still produces today's nudge, marked as late; with no sync by noon, the day gets no nudge. Reminders on consecutive days point to a broken automation, so flag them to yourself. |
| R2 False positives from periodic patterns | Weekly schedule and monthly rhythms shift sleeping HR and HRV on a predictable cycle. Without decomposition the detector fires on them, causing nudge fatigue and lost trust. | Quiet by default: scaled-MAD metric flags (D11) are already uncommon, and the readiness thresholds (D24) should keep ease off and rest uncommon too. Measure on your backfilled year how often each flag and each nudge would have fired. Consider a simple day-of-week adjustment in the MVP if weekly patterns dominate the flags. |
| R3 Timezone and day-boundary errors | Travel days produce corrupted daily aggregates and phantom anomalies. | Write the day-assignment rule explicitly (sleep to wake date, local time from record offsets) and unit-test it against a known trip. |
| R4 Export size and parse time (retired unless the fallback is used) | No export file is parsed in the MVP (D2). Applies only if the Shortcuts backfill fails and a one-time export plus converter script is needed as a fallback; your five-year export would likely exceed a gigabyte of XML. | If the fallback is triggered: a streaming parser (iterparse) in a local converter script that writes the same JSON sample format the Shortcut posts (D18). |
| R5 HRV sparsity and missing nights | SDNN readings are few and mostly overnight; a night off the wrist leaves the score with a missing input. | Score degrades gracefully (D35): a missing reading's weight moves to the other two and the card says it used 2 of 3 readings; with two or more missing, or no sleep recorded, the day gets no status. |
| R6 Duplicate and overlapping sources | When more than one device writes the same metric, naive processing double counts or mixes readings of different quality. | Watch only as the source for every MVP metric (D10), which sidesteps most overlap; log and ignore readings from other sources; if daytime metrics such as steps are added later, use interval-union summing tested against Apple's own daily totals. |
| R7 Health-adjacent claims | "Illness warning" and "rest today" carry responsibility if wrong or over-read. | Define the language and a clear not-medical-advice position before the first user sees a flag. |
| R8 Privacy and security of health data | You hold other people's health data on a server you operate. ## 6. Decisions You Still Need to Make

### Decided so far

- **D1** MVP users are you plus two or three testers. Consequences: user scoping, consent and basic security move into the must-haves; tester onboarding is needed; validation improves from one person to three or four.
- **D2** Revised: automated sync through an iPhone Shortcuts automation, for both the history backfill and the daily data. There is no manual export in the MVP. Consequences: no app to build; per-user upload tokens become a must-have (R19); the converter script drops out of the MVP and survives only as a fallback if the backfill spike fails; reliability of the automation (R9) becomes the main risk to a daily nudge; backfill depth is set at one year (D26).
- **D3** Data lives and computes on a hosted website you operate (revised twice: your laptop, then each tester's laptop, now hosted). Consequences: consent and security at full strength (R8); multi-user isolation as a correctness requirement (R13); hosting cost and operations (R14); usage evidence comes straight from server logs.
- **D4** Revised: the nudge is sent as a push notification each morning and shown on the card; tapping the notification opens the card. Consequences: push subscriptions, permission and a sending function become must-haves; the nudge reaches testers without them opening the app (R10 largely mitigated); lock-screen privacy (R23) and push reliability (R22) become risks; the morning nudge, and an 8pm follow-up only on days the nudge asked for a change (D32); on a morning with no sync by 11:30am, one reminder push comes first and the nudge follows if a sync arrives before noon (D34), so such a day can reach three pushes.
- **D5** Revised: readiness score built from sleeping HR, overnight HRV and sleep duration. Sleeping HR replaces Apple's resting HR because Apple's value leaves out sleep and is published once, at the end of the day, so at a morning sync the newest one is yesterday's daytime figure. Consequences: all three score inputs now describe last night; simplest inspectable score; no device dependency in the core signal; sleeping HR is derived by the MVP's own SQL from overnight heart-rate samples, so its correctness rests on the pandas check (R16); Apple's resting HR stays in the sync for the illness composite only.
- **D6** Rule-based score with fixed weights. Consequences: explainable from day one, trivially expressed in SQL and checked against the pandas reference (R16); the same weights for every tester keeps evidence comparable; initial weights and thresholds become a decision (D24); tunable or learned weights are design-for-later through stored parameters.
- **D11** Baseline defaults fixed for everyone: a 28-day window that never includes the day being judged, a flag at 2 scaled MADs (MAD multiplied by 1.4826, so it matches a standard deviation), and at least 21 valid days in the window before any flag. Consequences: simple and comparable across testers; per-metric flags are uncommon, so each one carries weight; values stored as settings, so they can change later without a rewrite.
- **D8** Illness and strain composite, weekly digest and nudge feedback all in. Consequences: R7 wording work is now on the must-do list; ingestion carries a few metrics beyond the score; digest generation approach becomes D22.
- **D22** Weekly digest written from a fixed template for the MVP, with a guarded model-written version as a later feature. Consequences: no language-model dependency or wording risk in the MVP; the digest job should be built so the writer is one swappable function.
- **D23** Digest is an in-app page only. Consequences: no email dependency or contact consent; the digest does not leave the app, although the daily nudge now does (D4); reach is bounded by visits, so the digest is a reason to return rather than a reminder to.
- **D24** Card shows status and top drivers, with the Why today screen one tap away. Consequences: the real card can be built from the start rather than a placeholder; opens of that screen are logged as a trust signal; the starting weights and thresholds still wait for the backfill spike.
- **D7** The causal layer is out of the MVP. The method is presented as design, and the experiment registry and event annotation schemas stay in the architecture so it can be built without rework.
- **D12** Daily sync cadence. Consequences: delivered by the Shortcuts automation (D2, D16) rather than by testers doing anything; the endpoint must be idempotent and incremental; the automation's reliability (R9) is what decides whether daily holds.
- **D16** Daily sync runs from two personal automations on each iPhone: a wake trigger when the phone is unplugged from its charger, and a catch-up trigger when the tester first opens an everyday app they choose. The Shortcut acts only between 4am and noon and stops if today's sync already succeeded, so daytime unplugs do nothing. Consequences: no reliance on the iPhone alarm; the wake trigger usually fires within minutes of getting up, and the catch-up guarantees an unlocked run (R9); the server's unfinished-night check (R24) and one-push-per-day rule make the pair safe; nudge timing varies a little by tester and day; onboarding covers two automations and choosing the catch-up app; overnight charging must be confirmed for each user (A11).

Consequences: the one-push-per-day rule becomes one morning nudge plus this follow-up, sent on every change day; the evening push carries no health detail (R23); the server needs each tester's local time; answers arrive the same evening, so the Value evidence stays fresh and complete; a tester who trains in the morning waits until 8pm to answer; train-as-planned days are never asked.

### Reversal and its consequences

The tool moved from a local app on each tester's laptop to a hosted website with login. This is a clean resolution of the earlier laptop-versus-card-only conflict and it matches the product brief, but it reverses the privacy benefit of local-first: other people's health data is now on a server you run, so consent and security return as must-haves at full strength (R8), multi-user isolation becomes a correctness requirement (R13), and hosting adds cost and operational load (R14). In exchange you get direct usage evidence without returned files, one version for everyone, and a demo that works from any browser.

### Confirmed: score and composite inputs

D5 and D8 pulled slightly against each other, since a composite built from the same three score metrics would add little. Confirmed resolution: the readiness score uses sleeping HR, HRV and sleep duration only (D5, revised); the illness and strain composite also reads Apple's resting HR (yesterday's daytime value) and respiratory rate where available; wrist temperature is excluded from the MVP entirely. Each day therefore carries up to five values, and the Shortcut needs to produce them.
