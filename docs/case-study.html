AI product management case study · Two test weeks done · November 2026

Clarivi

A plain-words morning answer from my own Apple Watch data

For Apple Watch users who train and sleep with the watch on. It says what last night's readings mean against your own normal, why the day's status was set, and one thing to do today. I built it and tested it on myself (I wrote the specs and decisions; Claude Code wrote the code to them), and I report what missed next to what worked.

The problem

Apple Watch records the numbers, and the Health app shows each as an isolated daily card. It does not say whether today's reading is unusual for you, or why.

Clarivi explains them, in plain words and against the user's own normal, which Apple's own features do not.

What I built

  • The card: Ready, Ease off or Rest in a few plain sentences, one action for the day, and a "Why today" screen that shows how it was decided.
  • The score: fixed arithmetic in SQL against my own last 42 nights, checked by separate pandas code. A night with sleep far below my usual cannot be Ready.
  • The AI (added after week one): Claude Opus 5.5 writes the wording and never sets the status. It sees an allow-list of results only, and its text goes through a filter, a retry and a fixed fallback.
  • The sync: for both test weeks, an iPhone Shortcut on three triggers (alarm stopped, charger unplugged, app opened), with an 11:30 reminder. Since 10 November, a native iPhone app that reads Health on its own.
  • Private by default: row-level security on every table, no third-party scripts, three separate consent boxes.

Key decisions

  • A fixed score, not a learned model: every status is explainable and checkable. The cost: it cannot adapt to me.
  • The AI explains, never decides: a wrong sentence cannot change what the card says. The cost: a short wait, and a US AI provider that needs its own consent.
  • A smaller test, set before any result: four people for 28 days became one person for a week. The cost: it cannot speak for anyone else.
  • From a Shortcut to an iPhone app: week two met the sync target only by leaning on four triggers and a reminder, and my own account said it still needed me. The cost: Swift to maintain and, on a free Apple ID, a reinstall every 7 days.
  • Test before building: I tried three planned analysis modules on my own year first. Two found nothing the data could support (0 of 12 each) and the third missed its target, so I built none.

What I learned

  • The sync miss was partly the Watch: its sleep can reach Health hours after waking. A fixed wait failed on its first real morning, so I dropped it and made the reminder cover an unfinished night.
  • A Ready on a very short night led to a sleep floor. It made the score louder than my "quiet" target (52 of 231 days, against 29 of 231 before it), and I report that as a miss.
  • Counting the sync three ways showed that "4 of 7" counted any post, so week two counted the status shown before noon, and met the target.
  • My own account after week one: I would not keep using it as it was. After week two: the status still needed me to sync by hand or stop an alarm I don't set on most days. That is why the sync is now an iPhone app.
  • The week's one Ease off fell on the morning my phone was off, so the action target is still untested.
